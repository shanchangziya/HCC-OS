# Proposed revision Methods

We first audited the archived candidate-gene objects, model objects, training code and cohort-specific risk scores. The positive single-cell OS-high marker set comprised 657 genes. Intersecting these markers with available genes in TCGA, GSE14520 and ICGC exactly reproduced the ordered 448-gene candidate list. The final legacy OSARS model contained 128 genes and 1,696 gradient-boosting trees. Legacy risk scores were retained without refitting. Because ICGC performance had been examined during the original model-comparison workflow, its results were treated as retrospective external-cohort evaluation, and TCGA legacy performance was explicitly designated apparent performance.

We separately evaluated a bounded TCGA-only training strategy using five outer folds and three inner folds, stratified by event status. The random seed was 20260905. Within each training partition, genes were winsorized at the training 1st and 99th percentiles, missing values were replaced by training means, and expression was standardized using training means and sample standard deviations. Independent univariate Cox models with Efron handling of ties selected genes at Wald P<0.01. Gene selection and preprocessing were repeated in every inner training partition and repeated in each outer training partition after tuning. Eight predefined configurations were compared: ridge Cox models with penalties 1 and 10; elastic-net Cox models with L1 ratio 0.5 and penalties 0.01 and 0.1; and Cox gradient-boosting models with tree depths 1 or 3 and 100 or 400 trees, learning rate 0.03, minimum leaf size 10 and subsampling fraction 0.5. The configuration with the highest mean inner-validation Harrell C-index was selected; ties followed the declared grid order. The primary estimate was the mean C-index across the five held-out outer folds. This analysis was conditional on the archived single-cell candidate set and did not repeat the original 117-configuration search or the single-cell discovery procedure. ICGC outcomes were not loaded during nested training. After outer evaluation, the same inner-selection procedure was applied to all TCGA data to fit one new model for retrospective ICGC evaluation.

Harrell C-indices and time-dependent AUCs were calculated on eligible samples with observed scores. For comparability with the archived timeROC results, cumulative/dynamic ROC calculations used marginal inverse-probability-of-censoring weights from reverse Kaplan–Meier estimation, events strictly before each horizon as cases, and observed survival strictly after each horizon as controls. Subjects at exactly the horizon were excluded from the case/control sets. Case weights used the censoring survival function immediately before the observed event time. Tied risk scores received half concordance. Primary horizons were one and three years. Five-year ICGC estimates were retained only as flagged audit outputs because two subjects remained beyond five years in the full cohort and none in the common benchmark subset.

Percentile 95% confidence intervals were obtained from 1,000 paired sample-row bootstrap resamples with seed 20260905. Each resample preserved all markers, outcomes and covariates for a sampled row; censoring weights were re-estimated for AUC. Paired differences used the same resamples for both markers. Fitted scores were held fixed, so these intervals characterize sampling uncertainty conditional on the existing fitted models and do not include training or selection uncertainty. For the mean outer-fold C-index, held-out rows were resampled within each outer fold and the resulting fold C-indices were averaged. Pooled OOF ROC and KM summaries were descriptive: scores were expressed as percentiles of the corresponding training-fold prediction distribution, and risk groups used the corresponding training-fold median. The legacy-apparent versus nested difference compared different strategies and was not interpreted as a pure optimism correction for the original GBM.

Two published oxidative-stress mRNA signatures were implemented using their reported coefficients without refitting: the eight-gene Hong–Cai signature (doi:10.1155/2022/6201987) and the three-gene Ma signature (doi:10.2147/JHC.S465592). For Hong–Cai, the score was 0.069×G6PD + 0.177×MT3 + 0.206×CBX2 + 0.063×CDKN2B + 0.078×CCNA2 + 0.164×MAPT + 0.248×EZH2 + 0.213×SLC7A11, applied to archived log-scale expression. For Ma, the score was −0.1655×PDGFD + 0.2184×G6PD + 0.2398×ADAM9, using within-cohort gene z-scores of log-scale expression. These were harmonized formula implementations because the original reports did not fully specify expression preprocessing. Alternative log/z-score implementations were examined as sensitivities. Missing raw ICGC values were not set to zero or imputed. The main three-model comparison used the same 343 TCGA samples and the same 141 ICGC samples with complete observed expression for both published formulas. Results from the full ICGC cohort were reported separately when the required genes were observed. The seven-gene Wang–Liu signature was documented as not evaluable from the inspected main text because exact numeric coefficients were unavailable.

Overall survival was summarized using Kaplan–Meier curves, log–log 95% confidence intervals, numbers at risk and two-sided log-rank tests. High risk was defined as a score strictly above the cohort median; equality was assigned to Low. Applying the frozen TCGA median to ICGC was a threshold-transfer sensitivity. Median follow-up and its confidence interval were estimated with reverse Kaplan–Meier methods. Cox models estimated the association of the score per cohort standard deviation with survival, adjusting for age per ten years, sex and early versus advanced stage in TCGA/ICGC. Complete cases were analyzed. The protein cohort adjustment included age and sex because stage was unavailable. Scaled Schoenfeld residual tests assessed proportional hazards. Three-degree-of-freedom natural splines were compared with linear score terms using likelihood-ratio tests; spline effect curves were referenced to the complete-case median. ICGC stage-stratified models and a TCGA OSARS-by-log(time/365) interaction were reported as assumption-driven sensitivities. Clinical increment models using ordinal stage alone, stage plus ROS, and stage plus frozen OSARS were fitted in TCGA and transferred with fixed coefficients to ICGC.

The protein analysis used the archived correlation-weighted OSARS protein surrogate, a distinct quantity from the GBM mRNA score. Official PDC000198 biospecimen mapping identified a tissue-label discrepancy for T724. Primary survival analyses therefore excluded T724 and retained 158 verified tumor-labeled observations; the original 159-row analysis was retained as an archival sensitivity. No sample substitution or protein-score refitting was performed. Recorded months were converted to days using 365/12 days per month. Both OS and RFS were retained, including null RFS results.

For bulk linkage, expression genes common to TCGA and ICGC defined a fixed 11,222-gene universe. Mean within-sample fractional expression ranks, with average ranks for ties, summarized the frozen 49-gene Hallmark ROS set, discovery MP4, and MP4 after removing ROS-overlap genes. Available gene counts were 45, 55 and 53, respectively; ATOX1 and TXN were the two ROS–MP4 overlaps. Spearman correlations with frozen OSARS and sample-bootstrap confidence intervals were calculated. Partial rank correlations were estimated by correlating residuals from rank regressions: continuous adjustment variables were ranked, stage was represented by indicator variables, and complete cases were used. Adjustment sets included ROS, ROS plus stage, and, in TCGA, ROS plus stage plus ESTIMATE tumor purity. Bootstrap samples repeated ranking and residualization. Approximate two-sided t-test P values and Benjamini–Hochberg correction across the 14 defined linkage comparisons were reported. These analyses assess association and do not establish a cell-intrinsic mechanism.

To address adjustment for both tumor stage and histological grade, we retained the previously fitted TCGA sensitivity model including frozen OSARS per cohort standard deviation, age per ten years, sex, stage III/IV versus I/II, and grade G3/G4 versus G1/G2. Grade was matched by unique sample ID from the archived clinical figure source. Complete-case analysis included 309 samples and 104 deaths. The score association was HR 4.2427 per SD (95% CI 3.4124–5.2750; P=1.13×10⁻³⁸). The score-specific Schoenfeld test indicated nonproportionality (P=0.0283; global P=0.2650), so the HR was interpreted as an average training-apparent association summary rather than a constant effect across follow-up. This sensitivity did not modify the primary model or model-search procedure.

Before considering a competing-risk analysis of recurrence in PDC000198, we audited the archived OS and RFS time/status fields. Both pairs were available, but a validated three-state event code, separate event dates and the original recurrence/death endpoint definitions were not available in the examined source table. Seventeen of 158 verified tumor observations had a recorded death with no recorded RFS event, while one observation had an RFS time later than its recorded OS censoring time. These findings require endpoint-definition and follow-up reconciliation before a joint recurrence/death process can be constructed. We did not infer unverified competing-event types or fit a Fine–Gray model; the reported RFS analysis must not be interpreted as a cumulative-incidence or competing-risk analysis.
